    Section 2.4 Linear Transformations

    Textbook section 2.6.
    🔗
    What makes a transformation a matrix transformation?
    🔗

    Definition 2.4.1.

    A transformation \(T:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^m\) is a linear transformation if
    🔗
    1. \(T({\mathbf{x}}+ {\mathbf{y}}) = \underline{T({\mathbf{x}}) + T({\mathbf{y}})}\) for every \({\mathbf{x}}, {\mathbf{y}}\in \mathbb{R}^n\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(T(c{\mathbf{x}})=\underline{c T({\mathbf{x}})}\) for every \({\mathbf{x}}\in \mathbb{R}^n\) and \(c \in \mathbb{R}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.4.2.

    Using \(\mathbb{R}=\mathbb{R}^1\text{,}\) \(T(x)=2x\) is a linear transformation, while \(T(x)=x^2\) is not.
    🔗
    Solution.
    For \(T(x)=2x\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} T(x+y) &= 2(x+y) = 2x + 2y = T(x) + T(y)\\ T(cx) &= 2(cx) = c(2x) = cT(x) \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    For \(T(x)=x^2\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} T(x+y) &= (x+y)^2 = x^2 + 2xy + y^2 \neq x^2 + y^2 = T(x) + T(y) \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.4.3.

    (a)

    If \(T\) is a linear transformation, what is...
    🔗
    \(T(\mathbf{0})\text{?}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(T(\mathbf{0}) = T(0 \mathbf{0}) = 0 T(\mathbf{0})\text{,}\) so \(T(\mathbf{0}) = \mathbf{0}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    \(T(-\mathbf{x})\text{?}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(T(-\mathbf{x}) = T((-1)\mathbf{x}) = (-1)T(\mathbf{x}) = -T(\mathbf{x})\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Recall: A vector \({\mathbf{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^n\) is a linear combination of vectors \({\mathbf{x}}_1, {\mathbf{x}}_2,\ldots {\mathbf{x}}_k\) if there are scalars \(a_1, a_2, \ldots, a_k\) such that
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} {\mathbf{y}}=a_1{\mathbf{x}}_1+a_2{\mathbf{x}}_2+\cdots a_k{\mathbf{x}}_k. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 2.4.4.

    If \(T:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^m\) is a linear transformation, then
    🔗
    \(T(a_1{\mathbf{x}}_1+a_2{\mathbf{x}}_2+\cdots a_k{\mathbf{x}}_k)= \underline{a_1T(\mathbf{x}_1) + a_2T(\mathbf{x}_2) + \cdots + a_kT(\mathbf{x}_k)}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Activity 2.4.1.

    Suppose \(T:\mathbb{R}^2\to\mathbb{R}^3\) is a linear transformation with \(T\left(\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2\end{bmatrix}\right)=\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 0 \\ 3\end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) \(T\left(\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\0\end{bmatrix}\right)=\begin{bmatrix} 4\\ 1 \\ 2\end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) Find \(T\left(\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4\end{bmatrix}\right)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Need to write \(\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}\) as a linear combination of \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}\) and \(\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Let \(a,b\) be scalars such that: \(\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} = a\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} + b\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\) This gives us the system:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} a - b &= 3 \\ 2a &= 4 \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    From the second equation: \(a = 2\) Substituting into first equation: \(2 - b = 3\text{,}\) so \(b = -1\)
    🔗
    Therefore: \(\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} = 2\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\)
    🔗
    By linearity of \(T\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} T\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} &= 2\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 0 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 0 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -6 \\ -1 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 2.4.5.

    Let \(T:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^m\) be a transformation.
    🔗
    • \(T\) is a linear transformation if and only if it is a matrix transformation.
      🔗
      🔗
    • In this case, \(T=T_A\) is the matrix transformation induced by the unique matrix \(A\text{,}\) given in terms of its columns by
      🔗
      \begin{equation*} A=\begin{bmatrix} T(\mathbf{e}_1) & T(\mathbf{e}_2) & \cdots & T(\mathbf{e}_n) \end{bmatrix}, \end{equation*}
      where \(\mathbf{e}_1,\mathbf{e}_2, \ldots \mathbf{e}_n\) are the standard basis vectors of \(\mathbb{R}^n\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof (if time).

    First show that if \(T\) is a matrix transformation, then it is linear: If \(T = T_A\) for some matrix \(A\text{,}\) then: \(T(\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{y}) = A(\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{y}) = A\mathbf{x} + A\mathbf{y} = T(\mathbf{x}) + T(\mathbf{y})\text{,}\) \(T(c\mathbf{x}) = A(c\mathbf{x}) = cA\mathbf{x} = cT(\mathbf{x})\)
    🔗
    Now show that if \(T\) is linear, then it is a matrix transformation: Let \(A = \begin{bmatrix} T(\mathbf{e}_1) & T(\mathbf{e}_2) & \cdots & T(\mathbf{e}_n) \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) For any \(\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ \vdots \\ x_n \end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) we can write: \(\mathbf{x} = x_1\mathbf{e}_1 + x_2\mathbf{e}_2 + \cdots + x_n\mathbf{e}_n\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Then by linearity: \(T(\mathbf{x}) = T(x_1\mathbf{e}_1 + x_2\mathbf{e}_2 + \cdots + x_n\mathbf{e}_n)\) \(= x_1T(\mathbf{e}_1) + x_2T(\mathbf{e}_2) + \cdots + x_nT(\mathbf{e}_n)\) \(= A\mathbf{x}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Therefore \(T = T_A\) is a matrix transformation.
    🔗
    For uniqueness, suppose \(T = T_B\) for some other matrix \(B\text{.}\) Then: \(A\mathbf{e}_i = T(\mathbf{e}_i) = B\mathbf{e}_i\) for all \(i\text{.}\) Therefore the columns of \(A\) and \(B\) are identical, so \(A = B\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Corollary 2.4.6.

    If \(T:\mathbb{R}^k\to\mathbb{R}^n\text{,}\) and \(S:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^m\) are linear transformations, then \(S\circ T\) is a linear transformation.
    🔗
    🔗

    Activity 2.4.2.

    Consider the linear transformation \(T:\mathbb{R}^2\to\mathbb{R}^2\) defined by \(T\left(\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix}\right)=\begin{bmatrix} 3x + \frac{3}{2}y \\ x + 2y \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) Find the matrix \(A\) such that \(T = T_A\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    To find the matrix \(A\text{,}\) we need to find the images of the standard basis vectors under \(T\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \(T(\mathbf{e}_1) = T\left(\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\right) = \begin{bmatrix} 3(1) + \frac{3}{2}(0) \\ 1(1) + 2(0) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\)
    🔗
    \(T(\mathbf{e}_2) = T\left(\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\right) = \begin{bmatrix} 3(0) + \frac{3}{2}(1) \\ 1(0) + 2(1) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{3}{2} \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}\)
    🔗
    Therefore, by the theorem, the matrix is:
    🔗
    \(A = \begin{bmatrix} T(\mathbf{e}_1) & T(\mathbf{e}_2) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & \frac{3}{2} \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.4.8.

    (a)

    Define \(T:\mathbb{R}^2\to\mathbb{R}^3\) by \(T\left(\begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix}\right)=\begin{bmatrix} x_1 +x_2 \\ x_1 \\ 3x_2 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) Show \(T\) is a linear transformation.
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(T(\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{y}) = \begin{bmatrix} (x_1+y_1)+(x_2+y_2) \\ x_1+y_1 \\ 3(x_2+y_2) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} x_1+x_2 \\ x_1 \\ 3x_2 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} y_1+y_2 \\ y_1 \\ 3y_2 \end{bmatrix} = T(\mathbf{x}) + T(\mathbf{y})\)
    🔗
    \(T(c\mathbf{x}) = \begin{bmatrix} cx_1+cx_2 \\ cx_1 \\ 3cx_2 \end{bmatrix} = c\begin{bmatrix} x_1+x_2 \\ x_1 \\ 3x_2 \end{bmatrix} = cT(\mathbf{x})\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Find \(A\text{,}\) where \(T=T_A\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(A = \begin{bmatrix} T(\mathbf{e}_1) & T(\mathbf{e}_2) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Is there an \(\mathbf{x}\) in \(\mathbb{R}^2\) with \(T(\mathbf{x})=\begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ -1 \\ 6\end{bmatrix}?\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    We need to solve \(A\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ -1 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix}\) This gives the system: \(x_1 + x_2 = 4\text{,}\) \(x_1 = -1\text{,}\) \(3x_2 = 6\)
    🔗
    From third equation: \(x_2 = 2\text{.}\) From second equation: \(x_1 = -1\text{.}\) This does not satisfy first equation: \(-1 + 2 = 1 \neq 4\)
    🔗
    Therefore, there is no such \(\mathbf{x}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
