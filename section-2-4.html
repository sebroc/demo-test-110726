    Section 2.5 Matrix Inverses

    Textbook section 2.4.
    🔗

    Definition 2.5.1.

    An \({n \times n}\) matrix \(A\) is called nonsingular, or invertible, if there exists an \({n \times n}\) matrix \(B\) such that \(AB = BA = I_n\). Such a \(B\) is called the inverse of \(A\text{.}\) If no such \(B\) exists, \(A\) is called singular or noninvertible.
    🔗
    Idea: The inverse of a matrix mimics the reciprocal of a real number.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.5.2.

    Let \(A = {\left[ \begin{array}{rr} 1 & 3 \\ 1 & 4 \end{array} \right]}\) and \(B = {\left[ \begin{array}{rr} 4 & -3 \\ -1 & 1 \end{array} \right]}\text{.}\) Compute both \(AB\) and \(BA\text{,}\) and make a conclusion using the language of inverses.
    🔗
    Solution.
    \begin{equation*} AB = \left[\begin{array}{cc} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{array}\right] = I_2 \quad\text{and}\quad BA = \left[\begin{array}{cc} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{array}\right] = I_2 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    \(\text{Therefore, } B \text{ is the inverse of } A.\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Fact: The inverse of a matrix, if it exists, is unique. Therefore, we can write \(A^{-1}\) for the inverse of \(A\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Fact: If \(AB = I_n\text{,}\) then \(BA = I_n\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Example 2.5.3.

    Does \(A=\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\) have an inverse?
    🔗
    Solution.
    Let’s suppose \(A\) had an inverse \(B = \begin{bmatrix} x & y \\ z & w \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) Then:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} AB &= I_2\\[5pt] \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} x & y \\ z & w \end{bmatrix} &= \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    This gives us the system of equations:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} 2x + z &= 1\\ 2y + w &= 0\\ 0x + 0z &= 0\\ 0y + 0w &= 1 \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The last equation states that \(0 = 1\text{,}\) which is impossible.
    🔗
    Therefore, \(A\) cannot have an inverse. This means \(A\) is singular.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Theorem: If both \(A\) and \(B\) are nonsingular \({n \times n}\) matrices, then the matrix \(AB\) is nonsingular and its inverse is \((AB){^{-1}}=\)\(B^{-1} A^{-1}\).
    🔗

    Proof.

    First, \((AB)(B^{-1}A^{-1}) = A(BB^{-1})A^{-1} = A I_n A^{-1} = AA^{-1} = I_n\)
    🔗
    Similarly, \((B^{-1}A^{-1})(AB) = B^{-1}(A^{-1}A)B = B^{-1} I_n B = B^{-1}B = I_n\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Follow up facts:
    🔗
    • If \(A_1, A_2,\ldots, A_k\) are \(n\times n\) invertible/nonsingular matrices, then \(A_1A_2\cdots A_k\) is invertible/nonsingular and \((A_1A_2\cdots A_k)^{-1}=\underline{A_k^{-1}\cdots A_2^{-1} A_1^{-1}}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • If \(A\) is invertible/nonsingular, then \(A^{-1}\) is invertible/nonsingular and \((A^{-1})^{-1}=\underline{A}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • If \(A\) is invertible/nonsingular, then \(A^T\) is invertible/nonsingular and \((A^{-1})^T=\underline{(A^T)^{-1}}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 2.5.4.

    (Inverse of \(2\times 2\) matrix) The \(2\times 2\) matrix
    \begin{equation*} A=\begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    is invertible if and only if \(ad-bc\neq 0\text{,}\) in which case
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} A^{-1}=\dfrac{1}{ad-bc}\begin{bmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    We’ll learn how to compute inverses for bigger matrices soon.
    🔗
    🔗

    Activity 2.5.1.

    Find the inverse of each matrix, if possible.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} A=\begin{bmatrix} -2 & -3 \\ 4 & 6 \end{bmatrix},\; B=\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 6 \\ 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    For \(A\text{:}\) \(\det(A) = (-2)(6) - (-3)(4) = -12 - (-12) = 0\text{,}\) so \(A\) is not invertible.
    🔗
    For \(B\text{:}\) \(\det(B) = (1)(3) - (6)(2) = 3 - 12 = -9 \neq 0\text{,}\) so \(B^{-1} = \frac{1}{-9}{\left[ \begin{array}{rr} 3 & -6 \\ -2 & 1 \end{array} \right]} = {\left[ \begin{array}{rr} -1/3 & 2/3 \\ 2/9 & -1/9 \end{array} \right]}\)
    🔗

    Linear Systems and Inverses.

    If \(A\) is an \({n \times n}\) matrix, then the linear system \(A{\mathbf{x}}= {\mathbf{b}}\) is a system of \(n\) equations in \(n\) unknowns. Suppose \(A\) is nonsingular. How can we use \({A^{-1}}\) to solve the system \(A{\mathbf{x}}= {\mathbf{b}}\text{?}\)
    🔗

    Solution.

    Multiply both sides of \(A{\mathbf{x}}= {\mathbf{b}}\) by \({A^{-1}}\) on the left:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} A{\mathbf{x}}&= {\mathbf{b}}\\ {A^{-1}}(A{\mathbf{x}}) &= {A^{-1}}{\mathbf{b}}\\ ({A^{-1}}A){\mathbf{x}}&= {A^{-1}}{\mathbf{b}}\\ I_n{\mathbf{x}}&= {A^{-1}}{\mathbf{b}}\\ {\mathbf{x}}&= {A^{-1}}{\mathbf{b}} \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Therefore, when \(A\) is nonsingular, \({\mathbf{x}}= {A^{-1}}{\mathbf{b}}\) is the solution to \(A{\mathbf{x}}= {\mathbf{b}}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Consequences:
    🔗
    • When \({A^{-1}}\) exists, then \(A{\mathbf{x}}= {\mathbf{b}}\) has a unique solution.
      🔗
      🔗
    • If \(A\) is invertible/nonsingular, then the ONLY solution to the homogeneous system \(A{\mathbf{x}}= {\mathbf{0}}\) is \({\mathbf{x}}=\underline{{\mathbf{0}}}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.5.5.

    Use the inverse of \(A\) to solve the linear systems \(A{\mathbf{x}}= {\mathbf{b}}\text{,}\) \(A{\mathbf{x}}= {{\mathbf{c}}}\text{,}\) and \(A{\mathbf{x}}= {\mathbf{0}}\text{,}\) where \(A\text{,}\) \({\mathbf{b}}\text{,}\) and \({{\mathbf{c}}}\) are given below.
    \begin{equation*} A = {\left[ \begin{array}{rr} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{array} \right]}, \quad {A^{-1}}= {\left[ \begin{array}{rr} -2 & 1 \\ \frac{3}{2} & -\frac{1}{2} \end{array} \right]}, \quad {\mathbf{b}}= {\left[ \begin{array}{r} 4 \\ 2 \end{array} \right]}, \ \text{ and } \ {{\mathbf{c}}} = {\left[ \begin{array}{r} 3 \\ 2 \end{array} \right]}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    For \(A{\mathbf{x}}= {\mathbf{b}}\text{:}\) \({\mathbf{x}}= A^{-1}{\mathbf{b}}= {\left[ \begin{array}{rr} -2 & 1 \\ \frac{3}{2} & -\frac{1}{2} \end{array} \right]}{\left[ \begin{array}{r} 4 \\ 2 \end{array} \right]} = {\left[ \begin{array}{r} -6 \\ 5 \end{array} \right]}\)
    🔗
    For \(A{\mathbf{x}}= {\mathbf{c}}\text{:}\) \({\mathbf{x}}= A^{-1}{\mathbf{c}}= {\left[ \begin{array}{rr} -2 & 1 \\ \frac{3}{2} & -\frac{1}{2} \end{array} \right]}{\left[ \begin{array}{r} 3 \\ 2 \end{array} \right]} = {\left[ \begin{array}{r} -4 \\ \frac{7}{2} \end{array} \right]}\)
    🔗
    For \(A{\mathbf{x}}= {\mathbf{0}}\text{:}\) \({\mathbf{x}}= A^{-1}{\mathbf{0}}= {\left[ \begin{array}{r} 0 \\ 0 \end{array} \right]}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Algorithm for Calculating Inverses.

    For a given \(n\times n\) matrix \(A\text{,}\) if we are looking for an inverse for \(A\) (call this matrix \(X\)), we must have \(AX=I_n\text{.}\) We are solving \(n\) linear systems simultaneously:
    🔗

    Solution.

    \(A\begin{bmatrix} x_{11} \\ x_{21} \\ \vdots \\ x_{n1} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ \vdots \\ 0 \end{bmatrix},\ A\begin{bmatrix} x_{12} \\ x_{22} \\ \vdots \\ x_{n2} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ \vdots \\ 0 \end{bmatrix},\ \cdots,\ A\begin{bmatrix} x_{1n} \\ x_{2n} \\ \vdots \\ x_{nn} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ \vdots \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Since, to solve each system, we wish to reduce the matrix \(A\) down to its RREF each time, we can combine all the systems into one big augmented matrix:
    🔗

    Solution.

    \([A \mid I_n] = \left[\begin{array}{rrrr|rrrr} a_{11} & a_{12} & \cdots & a_{1n} & 1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ a_{21} & a_{22} & \cdots & a_{2n} & 0 & 1 & \cdots & 0 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ a_{n1} & a_{n2} & \cdots & a_{nn} & 0 & 0 & \cdots & 1 \end{array}\right]\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Algorithm for computing \(A^{-1}\)
    🔗
    • Set up the augmented matrix \([A \vert I_n]\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Apply elementary row operations to \([A \vert I_n]\) to reduce it to \([I_n \vert{A^{-1}}]\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    If \(A\) is an \(n\times n\) matrix, either \(A\) can be reduced to \(I_n\) by elementary row operations or it cannot. In the first case, the algorithm produces \(A^{-1}\text{;}\) in the second case, \(A^{-1}\) does not exist.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.5.6.

    Find \({A^{-1}}\) where \(A = {\left[ \begin{array}{rrr} 1 & 1 & 4 \\ 2 & 3 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{array} \right]}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \([A \vert I_3] = {\left[ \begin{array}{rrr|rrr} 1 & 1 & 4 & 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 2 & 3 & 2 & 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 0 & 0 & 1 \end{array} \right]}\)
    🔗
    \(R_2 \to R_2 - 2R_1\text{:}\) \({\left[ \begin{array}{rrr|rrr} 1 & 1 & 4 & 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & -6 & -2 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 0 & 0 & 1 \end{array} \right]}\)
    🔗
    \(R_1 \to R_1 - R_2\text{:}\) \({\left[ \begin{array}{rrr|rrr} 1 & 0 & 10 & 3 & -1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & -6 & -2 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 0 & 0 & 1 \end{array} \right]}\)
    🔗
    \(R_1 \to R_1 - 10R_3, R_2 \to R_2 + 6R_3\text{:}\) \({\left[ \begin{array}{rrr|rrr} 1 & 0 & 0 & 3 & -1 & -10 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 & -2 & 1 & 6 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 0 & 0 & 1 \end{array} \right]}\)
    🔗
    Therefore: \(A^{-1} = {\left[ \begin{array}{rrr} 3 & -1 & -10 \\ -2 & 1 & 6 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{array} \right]}\)

    Box Of Facts/Invertibility Criteria
    🔗
    Let \(A\) be an \({n \times n}\) matrix. The following are equivalent:
    🔗
    1. \(A\) is nonsingular.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(A{\mathbf{x}}= {\mathbf{0}}\) has only the trivial solution.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(A\) is row equivalent to \(I_n\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. The linear system \(A{\mathbf{x}}= {\mathbf{b}}\) has a unique solution for every \(n \times 1\) matrix \({\mathbf{b}}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    5. \(\mathop{\mathrm{rank}}(A)=n\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗

    Inverses of Matrix Transformations.

    Let \(T=T_A:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^n\) be a matrix transformation induced by the \(n\times n\) matrix \(A\text{.}\) What does invertibility imply about the geometry of the transformation \(T_A\text{?}\)
    🔗
    Consider \(T'=T_{A^{-1}}\text{.}\) What happens when we compose them?
    🔗
    \(T\circ T'(\mathbf{x})=\underline{T_A(T_{A^{-1}}(\mathbf{x})) = T_A(A^{-1}\mathbf{x}) = A(A^{-1}\mathbf{x}) = (AA^{-1})\mathbf{x} = I_n\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}}\)
    🔗
    \(T'\circ T(\mathbf{x})=\underline{T_{A^{-1}}(T_A(\mathbf{x})) = T_{A^{-1}}(A\mathbf{x}) = A^{-1}(A\mathbf{x}) = (A^{-1}A)\mathbf{x} = I_n\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}}\)
    🔗

    Definition 2.5.7.

    Given a matrix transformation \(T:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^n\text{,}\) if there exists a transformation \(S:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^n\) such that \(S(T(\mathbf{x}))=\underline{T(S(\mathbf{x}))}=\underline{\mathbf{x}}\) for all \(\mathbf{x}\in \mathbb{R}^n\text{,}\) we say that \(T\) has an inverse.
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 2.5.8.

    Let \(T:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^n\) denote the matrix transformation induced by an \(n\times n\) matrix \(A\text{.}\) Then \(A\) is invertible if and only if \(T\) has an inverse. In this case, \(T\) has exactly one inverse (which we denote as \(T^{-1}\)), and \(T^{-1} : \mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^n\) is the transformation induced by the matrix \(A^{-1}\text{.}\) In other words
    \begin{equation*} (T_A)^{-1} = T_{A^{-1}} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    \(T\) and \(T^{-1}\) “reverse” or “undo” the action of each other.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.5.9.

    Consider \(T:\mathbb{R}^2\to\mathbb{R}^2\) given by reflection across the \(x\)-axis. Find the matrix representing this transformation, and find the inverse.
    🔗
    Solution.
    T is given by matrix \(A = {\left[ \begin{array}{rr} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{array} \right]}\)
    🔗
    Since \(A^2 = I_2\text{,}\) \(A\) is its own inverse, so \(T^{-1} = T\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.5.10.

    (a)
    Consider \(P:\mathbb{R}^2\to\mathbb{R}^2\) by \(P\left(\begin{bmatrix} a_1 \\ a_2\end{bmatrix}\right)=\begin{bmatrix} a_1 \\ 0\end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Is this a matrix transformation?
    🔗
    Solution.
    Yes, \(P\) is a matrix transformation. It can be represented by the matrix \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\)
    🔗
    To verify: \(P\left(\begin{bmatrix} a_1 \\ a_2\end{bmatrix}\right) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} a_1 \\ a_2\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} a_1 \\ 0\end{bmatrix}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    (b)
    Is this invertible?
    🔗
    Solution.
    No, \(P\) is not invertible. Note that \(\det(A) = 0\text{,}\) so \(A\) is not invertible.
