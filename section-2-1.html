    Section 2.1 Matrix Addition, Scalar Multiplication, and Transposition

    Textbook section 2.1.
    🔗
    We’ve seen matrices used to help us solve systems of linear equations. We’re now going to define them more formally and explore other ways to utilize them.
    🔗

    Definition 2.1.1.

    An \(m \times n\) matrix \(A\) is a rectangular array of \(m \cdot n\) real numbers arranged in \(m\) horizontal rows and \(n\) vertical columns:
    \begin{equation*} A = \begin{bmatrix} {a_{11}} & {a_{12}} & \cdots & \cdots & \cdots & {a_{1n}} \\ {a_{21}} & {a_{22}} & \cdots & \cdots & \cdots & {a_{2n}} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \cdots & \cdots & \cdots & \vdots \\ \vdots & \vdots & \cdots & \cdots & a_{ij} & \vdots \\ \vdots & \vdots & \cdots & \cdots & \cdots & \vdots \\ a_{m1} & a_{m2} & \cdots & \cdots & \cdots & a_{mn} \\ \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    The \(i\)th row of \(A\) is \(\begin{bmatrix} a_{i1} & a_{i2} & \cdots & a_{in} \end{bmatrix},\) and the \(j\)-th column of \(A\) is
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} {a_{1j}} \\ {a_{2j}} \\ \vdots \\ a_{mj}. \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    The number \({a_{ij}}\text{,}\) which is in the \(i\)th row and \(j\)th column of \(A\text{,}\) is the \((i,j)\)-entry of \(A\text{,}\) and we often write \(A = [{a_{ij}}].\) We say \(A\) is an “\(m\) by \(n\)” matrix.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.1.2.

    Consider the matrix
    \begin{equation*} A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 5 & -8 & -13 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    \(A\) is a 2 \(\times\) 3 matrix. Complete the following:
    🔗
    • \(\displaystyle {a_{11}} = \underline{1}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\displaystyle {a_{12}} = \underline{2}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\displaystyle {a_{21}} = \underline{5}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    • \(\displaystyle {a_{23}} = \underline{-13}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\displaystyle {a_{32}} = \underline{\text{no such entry}}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 2.1.3.

    Two matrices \(A\) and \(B\) are equal if they have the same size AND all the corresponding entries are equal.
    🔗
    🔗

    Activity 2.1.1.

    If \(A=\begin{bmatrix} 3 & y\\ 4 & -7\end{bmatrix}\) and \(B=\begin{bmatrix} 6+x & -2 \\4 & -7 \end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) and \(A=B\text{,}\) find \(x\) and \(y\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Since \(A = B\text{,}\) comparing corresponding entries:
    \begin{equation*} 3 = 6 + x \implies x = -3 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} y = -2 \end{equation*}
    Therefore \(x = -3\) and \(y = -2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Matrix Operations.

    Matrix Addition: If \(A = [{a_{ij}}]\) and \(B = [{b_{ij}}]\) are both \(m \times n\) matrices, then their sum \(A+B\) is the matrix \(C = [{c_{ij}}]\) where \({c_{ij}}=\) \({a_{ij}}+ {b_{ij}}\).
    🔗

    Example 2.1.4.

    For the matrices \(A\) and \(B\) as below, calculate \(A+B\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation*} A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 5 & -8 & -13 \end{bmatrix} \quad \text{and} \quad B = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 & -4 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    \begin{equation*} A+B = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 4 & 4\\ 6 & -5 & -17\end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 2.1.5.

    For \(A+B\) to be defined, \(A\) and \(B\) must be the same size. From now on, if we write \(A+B\text{,}\) assume that this is the case.
    🔗
    Scalar Multiplication: If \(A = [{a_{ij}}]\) is an \({m \times n}\) matrix and \(r\) is a real number, then the scalar multiple of \(A\) by \(r\text{,}\) written \(rA\), is the \({m \times n}\) matrix \(C = [{c_{ij}}]\text{,}\) where \({c_{ij}}=\)\(r{a_{ij}}\), that is, \(C\) is the matrix obtained by multiplying every entry of \(A\) by \(r\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.1.6.

    For \(A\) as in the example above, calculate \(-2A\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \begin{equation*} -2A = \begin{bmatrix} -2 & -4 & -6 \\ -10 & 16 & 26 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 2.1.7.

    If \(A_1, A_2, \ldots, A_k\) are \({m \times n}\) matrices and \(c_1, c_2, \ldots, c_k\) are real numbers, then an expression of the form
    \begin{equation*} c_1 A_1 + c_2 A_2 + \cdots + c_k A_k \end{equation*}
    is called a linear combination of \(A_1, A_2, \ldots, A_k\text{.}\) The scalars \(c_1, \ldots, c_k\) are called coefficients.
    🔗
    🔗

    Activity 2.1.2.

    The following is a linear combination of matrices. Compute it.
    🔗
    \(4 \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 2 \\ -3 & 3 \end{bmatrix} - \frac{1}{2} \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 6 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} &= \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 8 \\ -12 & 12 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 3 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} -2 & 7 \\ -15 & 11 \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Matrix Addition Properties.

    Let \(A\text{,}\) \(B\text{,}\) and \(C\) be \({m \times n}\) matrices.
    🔗
    1. \(A+B =\) \(A + B\), i.e. matrix addition is commutative.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(A + (B + C) =\) \((A + B) + C\), i.e. matrix addition is associative.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. There is a unique \({m \times n}\) matrix \(O\) such that \(A+O =\)\(A\) for any \({m \times n}\) matrix \(A\text{.}\) The matrix \(O\) is called the \({m \times n}\) zero matrix.
      🔗
      🔗
    4. For each \({m \times n}\) matrix \(A\text{,}\) there is a unique \({m \times n}\) matrix \(D\) such that \(A+D=\)\(O\). The matrix \(D\) must, in fact, be the matrix \(- A = (-1)A\). The matrix \(-A\) is called the negative of \(A\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Proof of (a) (if time).

    Let \(A = [a_{ij}]\) and \(B = [b_{ij}]\text{.}\) Then:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} A + B &= [a_{ij} + b_{ij}] \\ &= [b_{ij} + a_{ij}] \quad \text{(since real numbers are commutative)} \\ &= B + A \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Scalar Multiplication Properties.

    If \(r\) and \(s\) are real numbers and \(A\) and \(B\) are matrices of the appropriate sizes, then
    🔗
    1. \(r(sA)=\)\((rs) A\).
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \((r+s)A=\)\(rA + sA\).
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(r(A+B)=\)\(rA + rB\).
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Definition 2.1.8.

    If \(A = [{a_{ij}}]\) is an \({m \times n}\) matrix, then the transpose of \(A\text{,}\) denoted \(A^T = [{a_{ij}}^T]\text{,}\) is the \(n\) \(\times\) \(m\) matrix defined by
    \begin{equation*} {a_{ij}}^T = a_{ji} \end{equation*}
    In other words, the transpose of \(A\) is obtained by interchanging the rows and the columns of \(A\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.1.9.

    Compute the transpose for each of the given matrices:
    \begin{equation*} A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 5 & -8 & -13 \end{bmatrix}, \ \ B = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 2 & 3 \\ 6 & 2 & 3 \\ -1 & -2 & 3 \end{bmatrix}, \text{ and } C = \begin{bmatrix} 10 \\ 20 \\ 30 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(A^T = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 5 \\ 2 & -8 \\ 3 & -13 \end{bmatrix}\)
    🔗
    \(B^T = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 6 & -1 \\ 2 & 2 & -2 \\ 3 & 3 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\)
    🔗
    \(C^T = \begin{bmatrix} 10 & 20 & 30 \end{bmatrix}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 2.1.10.

    If \(A=[a_{ij}]\) is an \(m\times n\) matrix, the elements \(a_{11}, a_{22}, a_{33},\ldots\) are called the main diagonal of \(A\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Thus forming the transpose of a matrix \(A\) can be viewed as flipping \(A\) about its main diagonal.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Matrix Transpose Properties.

    If \(r\) is a scalar and \(A\) and \(B\) are matrices of the appropriate sizes, then
    🔗
    1. \((A^T)^T =\)\(A\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \((A + B)^T=\)\(A^T + B^T\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \((rA)^T=\)\(rA^T\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Proof of (b) (time permitting).

    Let \(A = [a_{ij}]\) and \(B = [b_{ij}]\text{.}\) Then \(A + B = [c_{ij}]\) where \(c_{ij} = a_{ij} + b_{ij}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    By definition of transpose: \((A + B)^T = [c_{ij}]^T = [c_{ji}]\)
    🔗
    Since \(c_{ij} = a_{ij} + b_{ij}\text{,}\) we have: \([c_{ji}] = [a_{ji} + b_{ji}] = [a_{ji}] + [b_{ji}] = A^T + B^T\)
    🔗
    Therefore, \((A + B)^T = A^T + B^T\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 2.1.12.

    A matrix \(A\) with real entries is called symmetric if \(A^T = A\). A matrix \(A\) is called skew symmetric if \(A^T = -A\).
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.1.13.

    Are the following matrices symmetric/skew symmetric/neither?
    🔗
    • \(\displaystyle A = {\left[ \begin{array}{rrr} 0 & 2 & -3 \\ -2 & 0 & 5 \\ 3 & -5 & 0 \end{array} \right]}, \quad B = {\left[ \begin{array}{rrr} 3 & 5 & 2 \\ 5 & 1 & 4 \\ 2 & 4 & -1 \end{array} \right]},\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\displaystyle C = {\left[ \begin{array}{rrr} 1 & 2 & -3 \\ -2 & 0 & 5 \\ 3 & 5 & 0 \end{array} \right]}, \quad D = {\left[ \begin{array}{rrr} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{array} \right]}.\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Let’s examine each matrix:
    🔗
    1. \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 2 & -3 \\ -2 & 0 & 5 \\ 3 & -5 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\) skew symmetric since \(A^T = -A\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(B = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 5 & 2 \\ 5 & 1 & 4 \\ 2 & 4 & -1 \end{bmatrix}\) symmetric since \(B^T = B\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(C = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & -3 \\ -2 & 0 & 5 \\ 3 & 5 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\) neither symmetric nor skew symmetric
      🔗
      🔗
    4. \(D = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\) both symmetric and skew symmetric
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 2.1.14.

    If a matrix \(A\) is symmetric AND skew symmetric at the same time, then \(A\) must be a zero matrix! (Why?)
